An AI chatbot can answer the routine after-hours dispatch calls that drivers make every night: appointment times, load and reference numbers, lumper and detention instructions, fuel and parking questions. It logs each answer against the load and wakes the on-call dispatcher only for breakdowns, rejected loads, safety issues and decisions that need a person.

## What do drivers actually call dispatch about after hours?

Ask any on-call dispatcher at a 30 to 200 truck fleet what their phone did last night and the list is remarkably consistent. Most calls are not emergencies. They are questions whose answers already exist somewhere in the TMS, the rate confirmation or yesterday's email thread:

- "What's the PO number for this pickup? The guard won't let me in without it."
- "My appointment says 0600 but the receiver says they have me at 0800. Which is it?"
- "I've been sitting here since 11. When does detention start, and who do I tell?"
- "Who pays the lumper, and do I need a receipt?"
- "Where am I supposed to park until the shipper opens?"
- "Did my BOL photos go through?"

A smaller share are the calls that do need a person at 2 AM: a breakdown, an accident, a refused load, a reefer alarm, a driver who is out of hours and needs a plan. The trouble is that both kinds of calls ring the same phone, and the person answering has to wake up, log in and dig through records for each one before they know which kind it is.

## Why are night calls a margin problem, not just a sleep problem?

The cost hides in three places.

**Detention that never gets billed.** According to the [American Transportation Research Institute](https://truckingresearch.org/2024/09/new-research-documents-substantial-financial-and-safety-impacts-from-truck-driver-detention/), drivers reported being detained (held more than two hours) at 39.3% of stops in 2023. Detention cost for-hire trucking more than 135 million hours, $3.6 billion in direct expenses and $11.5 billion in lost productivity that year. ATRI also found that while 94.5% of fleets charge detention fees, fewer than half of those invoices get paid. A detention claim is only as strong as its timestamps, and a lot of detention happens overnight, when the arrival time lives in a driver's text message and nobody writes it down until morning.

**Hours of service burned on hold.** Under the [FMCSA hours-of-service rules](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/regulations/hours-of-service/summary-hours-service-regulations), a property-carrying driver gets a 14-hour on-duty window. Every hour spent waiting for a callback about a gate code or an appointment change comes out of that window, and it can push the next load's pickup. UK hauliers and Canadian carriers run under their own driver-hours rules, but the arithmetic is the same: unanswered questions turn into lost driving time.

**Drivers who leave.** A Stay Metrics study of more than 62,000 drivers, [reported by Supply Chain Dive](https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/poor-communication-with-dispatchers-fuels-high-driver-turnover-rate/528287/), found that high dispatcher satisfaction was associated with 16% lower early turnover, and that 35% of drivers leave a company within their first three months. More recently, Platform Science's 2026 Driver Experience Report, [covered by The Trucker](https://www.thetrucker.com/trucking-news/business/platform-science-driver-experience-report-whats-really-causing-drivers-to-stay-or-leave), surveyed more than 1,100 drivers: 52% said technology influences whether they stay or leave, and the report puts the cost of each turned-over seat at roughly $13,000. A driver left waiting on a PO number at 3 AM remembers it. We covered the hiring side of this in [why fleets are putting AI agents on driver recruiting](/blog/ai-agents-trucking-driver-recruiting); keeping the drivers you already have is the cheaper half.

## How does an AI chatbot for trucking dispatch work overnight?

The chatbot sits where drivers already reach dispatch: a text line, a WhatsApp number, the driver app, or a voice line. It is connected to the systems that hold the answers (the TMS, load documents, customer facility notes, the fleet's own policies) and to the on-call rotation.

When a driver writes in, it identifies the driver and their active load, pulls the relevant record and answers in plain language. When the question is about something that changes the money, such as arrival at a facility, it records the timestamp and location against the load so detention has a clean paper trail by morning. When the message signals something a person must handle, it pages the on-call dispatcher with the load, the driver, the location and what has already been said, so the human starts the call already briefed.

Here is how a typical night compares:

| After-hours moment | Manual on-call phone | With an AI chatbot on the first line |
|---|---|---|
| Driver needs a PO or pickup number | Dispatcher wakes, logs in, searches email, then calls back | Answered from the load record in the same conversation |
| Appointment time conflict | Driver waits at the gate while dispatch tries to reach the customer | Chatbot shows the confirmed appointment, flags the conflict and notifies dispatch with both times |
| Driver arrives and waits | Arrival time sits in a text thread; detention clock is reconstructed the next day | Arrival logged with time and location; detention reminders sent at the customer's threshold |
| Lumper or fuel question | Callback, or the driver guesses and the receipt goes missing | Policy answer given, receipt photo requested and attached to the load |
| Breakdown or accident | Same phone, same queue as the PO questions | Escalated immediately to on-call with full context; routine questions stay off their phone |
| Morning handoff | Day dispatch reads a pile of texts and voicemails | A summary of every overnight exchange, sorted by load and by what needs action |

The chatbot does not dispatch loads, negotiate with customers or decide whether a driver should run. Those calls stay with your people. What changes is that your people only get woken for the conversations that need their judgment.

## What should the bot never answer on its own?

This is the question most fleets ask first, and it is the right one. A well-scoped dispatch chatbot has a short list of topics that always go to a human, regardless of how confident the system is:

1. **Safety and equipment.** Accidents, breakdowns, reefer temperature alarms, injuries, anything involving law enforcement.
2. **Hours-of-service judgment.** Whether a driver can legally take or continue a load is a compliance decision, so the chatbot surfaces the data and the dispatcher decides.
3. **Load changes with money attached.** Reroutes, refused freight, TONU situations, layovers.
4. **Anything emotional.** A frustrated driver at hour 13 needs a person, not a better answer.

Getting that line right for your fleet, your customers and your on-call rotation is where most of the design work sits. It depends on how your TMS stores facility notes, how your customers define detention, and what your dispatchers are comfortable delegating. That is a conversation about your operation, not a setting in a product.

## What results can a fleet reasonably expect?

We would not quote a percentage without seeing your call logs, and you should be wary of anyone who does. The questions worth answering from your own data are simpler:

- **How many after-hours contacts per night** reach dispatch, and what share are lookups versus real exceptions?
- **How much detention did you bill last quarter** compared with what your drivers say they sat through, especially on overnight appointments?
- **How long does a driver wait** for an answer between 10 PM and 6 AM today?

If most overnight contacts turn out to be lookups, or detention billed trails detention incurred on overnight appointments, both are problems an AI chatbot addresses without adding headcount.
